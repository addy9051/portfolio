True-Texture — Fabric-Mismatch
Returns Intelligence

Fashion listings lie about fabric; customers find out on their skin; returns follow. True-Texture mines the tactile truth out of review text, cross-checks it against the material each listing claims — across a two-axis fibre × weave ontology — interrogates the returner at return time with an adaptive LLM concierge, and hands the seller an evidence-backed fix, down to "your supplier is likely substituting polyester for the listed cotton."

Key Terms
Plain-language definitions for the terms used throughout this page
Fabric ontology
The system's "ground truth" for materials: per fibre, how it should feel, its thermal / ideal-weather profile, and the adjectives that signal it's not what was claimed.
Fibre vs weave (two axes)
Fibre (cotton, polyester) governs warmth & substitution; weave / construction (satin, fleece, denim) governs surface feel & how much air it traps. A garment carries both.
Substitution signature
The tactile tell-tale a cheap fibre leaves when masquerading as a premium one — cotton reported as "shiny / slick" points to polyester.
Semantic filter
A local embedding model that keeps only the review sentences actually about fabric feel, scored against anchor phrases — an upgrade over keyword or topic-model matching.
Negation gate
A rule that stops "it was not scratchy" being counted as a complaint — the dominant false-positive the naïve keyword approach produced.
Returns concierge
The LLM agent that, at return time, asks 1–3 adaptive questions with tappable options, then emits a structured diagnosis — replacing the vague "reason for return" dropdown.
Response matrix (2×2)
Feel-problem × weather-problem → four cases, each with a distinct customer message and seller action (audit / quality / listing-fix / no-action).
Tool-use / forced function call
The concierge can only answer through two tools (ask_question, submit_diagnosis); after the question budget the model is forced to submit — so output is always structured.
Three-tier agent memory
Semantic (durable facts, JSON) · procedural (how-to-act, skill.md) · episodic (dated sessions, SQLite) — the CoALA agent-memory pattern.
Control-group experiment
Testing the visual-match idea against products nobody complains about — if flagged and clean score the same, the signal doesn't discriminate. It didn't.
RTO (Return-to-Origin)
India-specific: a COD order refused at the doorstep, never opened. A different problem from post-delivery fabric returns — deliberately out of scope here.
Provider abstraction
One duck-typed chat interface behind Groq, Gemini and AWS Bedrock — swapping LLM providers is a single env var, no code change.

Business Context
Why "the fabric feels wrong" is a first-order problem for a fashion marketplace
BA

Online fashion returns at 25–35% are among the highest of any retail category, and a large share are driven not by size but by "the product is different from the listing" — the colour, the drape, and above all the fabric feel. On a value-fashion marketplace this is the single most common stated return reason, fuelled by unbranded sellers, reseller-copied studio photos, and rampant fibre substitution (viscose sold as "cotton", polyester "art silk" sold as silk).

The reverse-logistics maths is brutal: a single return costs ₹200–400 all-in against a value-fashion order value of ₹300–600 — one fabric-mismatch return erases the margin of three-to-five good orders. And the marketplace's own return-reason codes are near-useless: customers click whatever option is fastest, so the true fabric-disappointment volume hides inside "didn't like it". Producing trustworthy root-cause data is itself the product.

Fashion e-commerce (India-first: Myntra · Flipkart · Meesho · Ajio · Amazon.in)

Is This Worth Solving? — Market Sizing
The analysis that came before a line of code: how big is the addressable slice, honestly
BA

Before building, I sized the opportunity — and deliberately looked for reasons not to build it. The verdict: worth building, and India is the better market for it than the US. In the US, fabric mismatch is the minority slice of a fit-dominated returns problem, already circled by Amazon's Fit Insights. In India it is closer to the main event.

Problem Statement & White Space
What everyone else does passively, and the active loop nobody runs
BADS

Amazon's Fit Insights already mines reviews for fabric sentiment — but passively, after the fact. Nobody interrogates the customer at return time with a material rulebook, separates fibre substitution from poor quality from wrong-weather usage, and feeds a specific corrective action back to the seller. That active loop is the white space.

1️⃣
Active diagnosis, not passive mining
Structured 1–3-question interview at the moment of return, with neutral option chips — not a review scraped weeks later, and not a dropdown the customer lies on.
2️⃣
Physics cross-check
The reported sensation is checked against what the claimed material should actually feel like — turning "didn't like it" into "claims cotton, feels like shiny polyester".
3️⃣
Closed loop to the seller
A specific, cheap action — verify the fibre, source premium-grade material, or clarify the ideal-weather guidance in the listing — routed by the case, not a generic alert.

The two-axis ontology is the core idea. Fibre and weave are independent: fibre (cotton, wool) drives warmth and what a cheap substitute feels like; weave (satin, fleece, denim) drives surface feel and how much air it traps. A garment carries both, so the diagnosis carries both — a satin reported as "rough" is a mismatch regardless of fibre, and a cotton fleece is warm even though cotton is breathable, because the construction traps heat. Weave weather profiles are web-grounded (open weaves → summer, pile weaves → winter).

Grounding, not trusting. The engine injects the material ground truth into every diagnosis deterministically — the seller always sees "claims cotton (soft/matte/breathable) → customer got rough/coarse", regardless of what the LLM chose to mention. When a listing states no fabric (61% of the sample), it falls back to the category's likely fibres so the agent can still probe intelligently.

Key Findings & Results
Calibrated, gated, verified live — including a negative result kept as a feature
DADS
9 in 10
flags are correct — up from about 1 in 2
The system only marks a review as a genuine fabric complaint when it's confident. I tuned that confidence cut-off on 48,000 real review sentences, which lifted the share of correct flags from roughly half to about nine in ten. It matters because a wrongly-flagged "scratchy" could get an innocent supplier audited — so it catches fewer complaints in exchange for trusting the ones it does catch.
3
guardrails so it's hard to fool into a false complaint
A gripe only counts if the customer truly meant it. Three checks: ignore negatives ("it was not scratchy"), only count unhappy reviews (3 stars or fewer), and require two separate reviews before raising a top-priority "audit this supplier" alert — one review saying "rough and scratchy" is a single opinion, not two.
4
look-alike return reasons it tells apart
"The fabric felt wrong" can mean four very different things, and the assistant works out which: the fabric is fake (a cheaper fibre swapped in), it's real but low-grade, the buyer wore it in the wrong weather, or nothing is actually wrong. Each gets the seller a different, specific action — all four tested on a live AI model.
98%
of the fabrics real listings name, it understands
The system knows 12 fibres (cotton, wool, polyester…) and 23 weaves (satin, denim, fleece…) — how each should feel and which weather it suits. Together they cover 98% of the materials listings actually mention; the small rest are vague words like "blended" that point to no specific fabric.

The concierge separates three look-alike causes — verified on live model runs

Customer says… Diagnosis Seller action
"shiny, cheap plastic, not cotton" Fibre substitution → polyester SUPPLY_CHAIN_AUDIT — verify / declare fibre
"rough, coarse, scratchy cotton" Low-grade genuine cotton QUALITY_IMPROVEMENT — source combed / long-staple
"felt fine, but I was cold outdoors" Wrong-weather usage (no defect) LISTING_FIX — clarify ideal weather; educate + layering tip
"fabric fine, mild day, just not my style" No fault found NO_ACTION — escalate to distributor only past a returns threshold

The weave axis genuinely overrides the fibre: a live run on a cotton/polyester fleece hoodie worn on a hot day flagged a weather mismatch even though the cotton fibre is hot-ideal — because the fleece construction traps heat. The model resolved the fibre-vs-weave conflict in favour of the construction, exactly as designed.

The honest negative result — a control experiment that killed the obvious idea

The tempting feature is "compare the listing photo to the customer photo with CLIP and flag big differences." I tested it properly — against a control group of products nobody complains about, in two conditions:

Condition Flagged (n=10) Control (n=10)
Whole frame CLIP 0.648 · colourΔ 0.689 CLIP 0.659 · colourΔ 0.598
Garment crops (rembg) CLIP 0.727 · colourΔ 0.711 CLIP 0.738 · colourΔ 0.686

The distributions overlap in both conditions — photo-level appearance comparison cannot see texture, and the popular "flag if CLIP < 0.75" heuristic fires on 20 of 20 products. So the visual channel is demoted to corroboration only (INCONCLUSIVE unless a product falls outside the entire control band), and text stays the primary signal by design. Keeping this negative result — rather than dressing up a detector that doesn't work — is the point.

Agent Memory Architecture (staged)
Three tiers — kept deliberately lightweight, infrastructure added only where the simple approach breaks
DS
Tier Implementation Why not more (yet)
Semantic — durable facts Fibre + weave ontology & category priors, injected as JSON Only a few KB; RAG over 35 facts is slower, costlier, and can retrieve-miss. Stays git-diffable.
Procedural — how to act Interview policy + response matrix in skill.md Loaded every turn, not retrieved — a versioned file separates policy from code.
Episodic — dated events Concierge sessions in SQLite (SQL recency by ASIN / date / case) Vector-store relevance is a later stage, once session volume justifies it.

The judgment that reads as senior here is knowing when not to reach for a vector database. A fixed 35-fact ontology injected into the prompt is faster, cheaper, and more reliable than RAG over it — and it keeps the curated ground truth auditable. Infrastructure earns its place only when the knowledge grows unbounded; the store already isolates persistence behind two functions so the upgrade is a drop-in.

Reflections & What I'd Do Differently
The honest section — including the cloud-account saga most write-ups would hide
DS
✓ What worked well
Grounding the diagnosis deterministically from the ontology, rather than trusting the LLM to recite facts — the seller always sees the claim-vs-experience contrast.
Running a real control-group experiment on the visual idea and keeping the negative result instead of shipping a detector that fires on everything.
The provider abstraction — when AWS and Google both blocked me at the billing layer, switching to Groq was one env var, and the whole pipeline kept running at $0.
↺ What I'd do differently
The demo dataset uses Amazon category strings, not the Indian taxonomy — the category-prior fallback is built and tested but won't auto-fire until a taxonomy mapper is added.
The fabric ontology is heuristic, not lab physics (GSM / RET values are rarely on listings) — fine for a prototype, but I'd label it an ontology, not an instrument.
Groq's free-tier token-per-minute cap makes multi-question sessions pause mid-run; a paid tier or a smaller model would smooth the live demo.

The cloud-account saga (a real lesson, not a footnote)

The concierge was designed for AWS Bedrock. Three walls, in order: Anthropic models on Bedrock are Marketplace-billed and not covered by promotional credits; the account then hit an AISPL (India billing entity) restriction returning ValidationException: Operation not allowed on every invoke; and a pivot to Google AI Studio hit a separate billing issue. The fix wasn't more code — it was recognising these as account-level walls and choosing a provider with no billing surface at all (Groq, sign-in-only free tier). The takeaway I'd carry into any real deployment: abstract the provider on day one, and treat "it works in the console" as no guarantee the API will.
